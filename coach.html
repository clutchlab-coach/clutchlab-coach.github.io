Step 2 of 5 · 60 seconds

Visualize

CC

Now the mind rehearses before the body has to. Have your athlete close their eyes and see one key play, done well — not the whole game, just one play.

For example: see yourself scanning the field, receiving the ball cleanly, and playing the pass you pictured — start to finish.

First 20 seconds — See where you are. The field, the sounds, your uniform, the ball.
Next 20 seconds — Play the moment in real time. Every move, exactly how you've practised it.
Last 20 seconds — Finish the play well. Feel how it feels when it comes off. Hold that feeling.

The questions every sports parent runs into

In the car, on the sideline, at the kitchen table — these six come up every season. Each answer is written straight from ClutchLab's own guide, car-ride-home cheatsheet, and workshop notes, with the exact lines to borrow. For your athlete's specific situation, leave a Question Ticket above — that one gets a personal answer by email.

After a mistake

“My daughter says ‘I'm so bad’ after every tough game. What do I say?”

Don't fix it, and don't argue with the feeling — she'll just stop telling you the truth. Listen first, then name the effort, not the score: “That was a tough one. I'm proud of how you kept going.” Then point forward: ask what the next play is. One hard game is data, not a verdict.

The car ride home

“He goes silent after a loss and won't talk about the game.”

Let him. Open with “I loved watching you play today” — then stop. Silence is fine; let him speak first. If he opens up, reflect it back (“That sounds really frustrating”) instead of coaching. If he doesn't: “That's fine. I'm here when you want to talk.” No replaying mistakes in the car.

Using the reset

“My son refuses to do the reset. Says it's embarrassing.”

That's exactly why it's designed to be invisible — breathing, one pictured play, one quiet word. Nothing anyone can see. Hand him the routine and let him own it; before a game, a one-line reminder is enough: “Do your reset.” The moment it becomes a lecture, it stops working. Calm, simple, pressure-free — that's the parent's whole job.

From the sideline

“What should I actually shout during the game?”

Cheer the effort, never coach the play. “Great hustle!” “Good effort — keep going!” “I love how hard you're working.” Instructions from the sideline (“Shoot! Pass! What are you doing?!”) split your athlete's attention between you and the game — and they're listening for your voice more than anyone's.

Before the game

“How do I pump him up without piling on pressure?”

Skip “You have to win today” and “Don't mess this up” — both make the game about your feelings. What lands instead: “I love watching you play.” “Have fun out there — I can't wait to see you play.” Then hand the nerves a job: “Remember your Reset: one slow breath, your cue word, your first job.”

Honest limits

“Is this therapy? Will it fix my child's anxiety?”

No, and we won't pretend otherwise. ClutchLab teaches practical mental skills for sport — routines, focus, self-talk, composure. It doesn't diagnose or treat anxiety or anything medical, and it makes no promises about results. If your athlete needs that kind of support, a licensed professional is the right call — and the reset can happily sit alongside it.
